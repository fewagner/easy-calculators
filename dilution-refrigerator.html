How pulse tubes and dilution refrigerators cool

Both machines use the same trick. A substance gets cold when it expands or evaporates, and gets hot when it is compressed. The machine arranges things so the cold part of the cycle happens next to your plate, and the hot part happens where the heat can be thrown away.

Pulse tube

Two everyday effects carry the whole idea:

  • Compressing a gas heats it. A bike pump gets warm when you use it.
  • Letting a gas expand cools it. A spray can gets cold when you spray.

The pulse tube compresses the gas while it sits at the warm end and lets it expand while it sits at the cold end.

Two helpers make this work:

  • The regenerator is a heat sponge. Gas passing through it on the way down leaves its heat in the sponge. Gas passing on the way back up takes that heat back. So the gas arrives at the cold end already cold, and leaves warm again.
  • The gas inside the tube acts as a piston made of gas. It slides up and down but never leaves the tube. The orifice and buffer set the timing so that this piston is pushed toward the warm end while it is hot, and back toward the cold end while it is cold.

Red arrows = heat flow, blue/gray dashes = gas flow, slug color = gas temperature

The four steps:

  1. Squeeze. The valve opens to high pressure. Fresh gas goes through the sponge, gives up its heat there, and pushes the gas piston up toward the warm end. The gas in the tube is compressed, so it heats up, like the bike pump.
  2. Throw heat away. The top of the gas piston is now hotter than the cooling water, so heat flows into the water. That gas is back at room temperature but still under pressure.
  3. Expand. The valve switches to low pressure. All the gas expands and cools, like the spray can. The gas piston slides back down, and the gas at the bottom becomes colder than your plate.
  4. Take heat from the plate. Heat flows from the plate into this colder gas. On its way back to the compressor, the gas collects the heat it left in the sponge in step 1. That leaves the sponge cold again for the next cycle.

Net result: each cycle carries a small amount of heat from the plate up to the cooling water. One cycle takes about a second. The 2nd stage runs the same cycle again, starting from the temperature the 1st stage reached.

Dilution refrigerator

The core idea is evaporative cooling, like sweat cooling your skin. The difference is that the ³He does not evaporate into a gas. It “evaporates” from one liquid into another liquid.

Below about 0.87 K, the mixture in the mixing chamber separates into two layers, like oil and water:

  • Top layer: almost pure liquid ³He. This plays the role of the liquid.
  • Bottom layer: a small amount of ³He dissolved in ⁴He. The ⁴He is superfluid and acts like empty space for the ³He, so the ³He atoms there behave much like a gas. This layer plays the role of the vapor.

When a ³He atom moves from the top layer into the bottom layer, it needs energy, just as a water molecule does when it evaporates. It takes that energy from its surroundings, which cools them.

Why it still works near zero kelvin: ordinary evaporation dies out at low temperature, because the vapor pressure drops to nothing. Here, even at absolute zero, the bottom layer can still hold about 6.6% ³He, so there is always room for more ³He to “evaporate” into.

Four-step animation of the helium-3 circulation loop in a dilution refrigerator: condensing, precooling, dilution in the mixing chamber, and evaporation at the still.

Orange = ³He-rich stream, blue = dilute phase, red arrows = heat flow

The four steps:

  1. Return and liquefy. The pump at room temperature sends ³He gas back into the fridge. On the way down it is cooled by the pulse tube stages at the 50K and 4K plates. Because it is under pressure, it turns into a liquid, like the gas in a lighter.
  2. Precool. The liquid flows down past the still and through the heat exchangers. Its pipe runs alongside the pipe of cold mixture going up, and passes its heat to it. By the time it reaches the mixing chamber, it is almost as cold as the mixing chamber.
  3. Evaporate into the other liquid (this is where the cooling happens). In the mixing chamber, ³He atoms cross from the top layer into the bottom layer. Each crossing takes a little heat out of the mixing chamber and your sample.
  4. Remove the ³He again (the still). If nothing else happened, the bottom layer would fill up with ³He and the cooling would stop, like sweat on skin in humid air. So the ³He has to be taken out of the bottom layer:
    • The bottom layer extends up a tube into the still. The still sits at about 0.8 K, and a small heater warms it.
    • At that temperature, ³He evaporates far more easily than ⁴He, so almost only ³He boils off. The pump takes it away.
    • The bottom layer in the still now has less ³He than the bottom layer in the mixing chamber. This difference pulls more ³He up from the mixing chamber, much like a straw pulls up liquid.
    • The pumped gas goes back to step 1.

In one sentence: the mixing chamber is where the ³He evaporates, the still is the fan that keeps clearing it away, and the pump and pulse tube recycle it.
